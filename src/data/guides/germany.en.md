## Germany in 2026: why it is now the most accessible of the four

Germany is the only country in this series that lets you enter **to look for work** on a proper residence permit rather than as a visitor. That distinction matters more than any single threshold: in Spain, Italy and France you need an offer or proven income *before* you move; in Germany you can arrive first and search afterwards.

Three developments explain the current position.

**The Opportunity Card (Chancenkarte)** has been running since **1 June 2024**, and is Germany's first points-based route in the Canadian or Australian sense. It gives you a year in the country to job-hunt, with the right to work 20 hours a week and to take **trial employment** with prospective employers.

**Blue Card thresholds rose about 5%** on **1 January 2026**: **EUR 50,700** for standard occupations and **EUR 45,934.20** for shortage occupations, recent graduates and IT specialists.

**The new citizenship law** (in force since **27 June 2024**) cut naturalisation to **five years** — three in cases of exceptional integration — and **permits dual citizenship**. For readers of this guide that is Germany's single biggest advantage: a Ghanaian, Kenyan or New Zealand citizen no longer has to renounce their original nationality, and the wait is half what Spain requires.

**But one condition governs every route: recognition of your qualification.** Germany does not treat your degree as a piece of paper but as a technical equivalence decided through the **anabin** database and a **ZAB** assessment. Start there, not with the visa.

## The main legal routes

### 1) Opportunity Card — Chancenkarte

A **one-year** residence permit, extendable to two, to look for work inside Germany.

**Two ways to qualify:**

**Route one — full recognition.** If your qualification is **fully recognised** in Germany, you get the card directly, with **no points required**. This is the fastest path and it is widely overlooked.

**Route two — the points system.** You need **at least six points**, after meeting two baseline conditions:
- a university or vocational qualification of **at least two years**, recognised in the country where you obtained it;
- **German at A1** or **English at B2**.

| Points criterion | Points |
| --- | --- |
| Partial recognition of the qualification | **4** |
| Occupation on the shortage list | **1** |
| Work experience: two of the last five years | **2** |
| Work experience: five of the last seven years | **3** |
| German at A2 / B1 / B2 | **1 / 2 / 3** |
| English at C1 | **1** |
| Age under 35 | **2** |
| Age 35 to 40 | **1** |
| Previous stay in Germany of six months or more | **1** |
| Spouse or partner who also qualifies | **1** |

**Proof of funds — the number that sinks applications.** The Opportunity Card requires **EUR 13,092** in a blocked account (*Sperrkonto*), at **EUR 1,091 a month for twelve months**. That is **higher** than the EUR 11,904 student visa amount. Depositing the student figure for an Opportunity Card application means refusal. A part-time employment contract covering your living costs is the accepted alternative.

**What the card permits:** part-time work up to **20 hours a week**, and **trial employment** with a prospective employer for up to two weeks. Once you have a suitable offer, you convert to a work permit or Blue Card inside Germany, without leaving.

### 2) EU Blue Card — Blaue Karte EU

The strongest route for graduates, and the fastest path to permanent residence.

| Category | Minimum gross annual salary 2026 | Monthly equivalent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard occupations | **EUR 50,700** | ~EUR 4,225 |
| Shortage occupations, graduates within 3 years, IT specialists | **EUR 45,934.20** | ~EUR 3,827 |

The two thresholds are 50% and 45.3% of the annual pension contribution ceiling, which is why they rise automatically each January. To see what those salaries actually buy, compare them with the [cost of living in Berlin](/en/explorer?to=de) and check [which German roles reach the threshold](/en/jobs?country=de).

**Why it is the first choice when available:**
- **Permanent residence (Niederlassungserlaubnis) after 27 months**, or **21 months** with German at B1.
- **Family reunification with no language requirement** for your spouse, who gets full work rights immediately.
- **Mobility within the EU** after 12 months.
- IT specialists can qualify **without a degree** by evidencing **three years** of relevant experience in the last seven.

### 3) Skilled Worker visa — §18a / §18b AufenthG

For applicants who already hold an offer, with a recognised academic (§18b) or vocational (§18a) qualification. There is no fixed salary floor as with the Blue Card, but the salary must be appropriate to the market, and approval from the **Federal Employment Agency (BA)** is required in most cases.

### 4) Recognition Partnership — Anerkennungspartnerschaft

A newer route, and a genuinely useful one if your qualification is only **partially** recognised: you enter Germany on a job offer and complete the recognition process **after arrival**, jointly with your employer, instead of waiting for it to conclude at home. For nurses, technicians and skilled trades in particular, this removes months from the timeline.

## Before anything else: get your qualification recognised

This is not a side formality. It is the gate every route above passes through.

1. **Look up your institution and award in [anabin](https://anabin.kmk.org)** — you want an **H+** rating for the university and an "equivalent" or "comparable" classification for the degree.
2. If it is not listed, request a **Statement of Comparability (Zeugnisbewertung)** from the **Central Office for Foreign Education (ZAB)**. Around EUR 200, typically **two to three months**, sometimes longer.
3. **Regulated professions** — medicine, nursing, pharmacy, engineering, teaching — are recognised by the **individual federal state**, not by ZAB, and the procedure differs between states.

**Timing advice:** begin recognition **six months** before you intend to apply. The most common source of delay for applicants from Ghana and Kenya is not visa refusal — it is arriving at a consular appointment with an unfinished assessment.

## Country-specific requirements

| Item | 🇬🇭 Ghana | 🇰🇪 Kenya | 🇦🇪 UAE | 🇳🇿 New Zealand |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| National (D) visa required in advance | **Yes** | **Yes** | Yes, for long stays | **No** (§41 AufenthV) |
| Application centre | VFS Global Accra | VFS Global Nairobi | Embassy / consulate | Handled inside Germany |
| Police certificate | CID Police Clearance | DCI Good Conduct | UAE police | NZ Police Certificate |
| Document legalisation | MFA Ghana + embassy | MFA Kenya + embassy | **UAE MOFA attestation** | Apostille from DIA |
| Document verification check | **Very likely** | **Very likely** | Rare | Rare |
| Typical timeline | 3–6 months | 3–6 months | 6–12 weeks | **Weeks, after arrival** |

### 🇬🇭 Ghana — applying in Accra

- **Appointments:** German national visa applications in Ghana run through **VFS Global in Accra**, booked online. National-visa slots are far scarcer than Schengen slots, so book as soon as your file is complete.
- **Police certificate:** the **Police Clearance Certificate** from the **CID** at Ghana Police headquarters, in the international-use format.
- **Legalisation:** notary → **Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Regional Integration** → German embassy consular section. Check Ghana's current status in the **Apostille Convention register** first; available sources conflict, and the difference between the two paths is weeks and fees.
- **Document verification (Urkundenüberprüfung):** the German embassy submits educational and civil documents from a number of countries to an authenticity check through a local trusted lawyer. It adds **six to twelve weeks** and extra cost. Plan for it rather than being surprised by it.
- **Language:** **Goethe-Institut** and **telc** certificates are the ones accepted in practice. The Goethe-Institut in Accra examines locally.

### 🇰🇪 Kenya — applying in Nairobi

- **Appointments:** through **VFS Global Nairobi**, with the file uploaded before attending.
- **Police certificate:** **Certificate of Good Conduct** from the DCI via **eCitizen**, with fingerprints.
- **Bank statement rules:** **six months**, stamped by the branch, with a formal covering letter. If you are funding through a **blocked account**, the document required is the **Sperrbescheinigung** from an approved provider — not your personal bank statement. Confusing the two is a common cause of delay.
- **Degree authentication:** **KNQA** and **CUE** for domestic recognition, then the **Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Diaspora Affairs**, then the German embassy. Run the German **anabin/ZAB** track in parallel — that is the one the immigration authority actually acts on.
- Kenyan nurses in particular: the **Recognition Partnership** is usually faster than waiting for full recognition before departure.

### 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates — applying from your country of residence

- **Consular jurisdiction:** you apply where you live rather than where you hold nationality, provided your **UAE residence permit is valid** for long enough — commonly three to six months beyond your intended travel date.
- **No-objection certificate:** an employer letter on letterhead with job title, salary, start date and consent to travel.
- **Bank evidence:** **six months** of stamped UAE bank statements plus a salary certificate. Regularity matters more than the closing balance.
- **Attestation:** **UAE MOFA** then the German embassy. Degrees issued by universities inside the UAE need **Ministry of Higher Education** attestation first.
- **UAE nationals:** exemption from short-stay Schengen visas does not remove the need for a **national (D) visa** for long stays.

### 🇳🇿 New Zealand — the strongest passport advantage in this series

- **Section 41 of the Residence Ordinance (§41 AufenthV):** New Zealand is one of the **privileged nationalities** that may **enter Germany without a visa and then apply for a residence permit from inside the country** at the Ausländerbehörde, within **90 days** of entry. No return trip to New Zealand, no consular appointment.
- **This covers work, study and job-seeking**, not just visits. In practice: arrive, register your address (*Anmeldung*), arrange health insurance and a bank account, then file.
- **The practical warning:** appointment waits at immigration offices in Berlin, Munich and Hamburg run to **weeks or months**. Book the appointment **on the day you arrive**, not once you have settled in. Filing within the 90 days is what protects your status even if the appointment falls later.
- **Apostille:** documents are legalised with a single **apostille** from the **Department of Internal Affairs**.
- **Working Holiday:** available at ages 18–30 for up to twelve months.
- **EES:** fully operational at Schengen borders since **10 April 2026**; fingerprints and a facial image are recorded and stay counting is automatic.
